Accused of electricity theft and your power was cut? The Supreme Court has explained your remedies ⚡⚖️

A Gujarat business was accused of electricity theft and billed about ₹93 lakh, plus about ₹23 lakh in compounding charges. The Gujarat High Court allowed it to deposit 50% of the bill and get its power reconnected. The power companies challenged this, and the Supreme Court dismissed their appeal.

The Court held that there is no separate procedure for theft bills. They must follow Section 126 of the Electricity Act, 2003: a provisional assessment, a chance to file objections, a hearing, and then a final assessment. The final assessment can be appealed under Section 127, which needs a deposit of half the assessed amount. If a criminal case follows, the Special Court decides final liability, and any excess deposit must be refunded.

But 50% is not an automatic right. Because theft involves criminal intent, High Courts must be careful. Where the provisional bill looks prima facie arbitrary, the High Court may, for recorded reasons, order supply restored on a deposit of at least 50%. In a suitable case, it can still ask for the full provisional amount.

Case: Dakshin Gujarat Vij Company Ltd. v. Vaibhav Metal Crusher, 2026 INSC 1157 (Supreme Court, Justices Manoj Misra and K.V. Viswanathan)
