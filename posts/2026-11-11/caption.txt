Is your grandfather's property automatically yours by birth? The Supreme Court says not always 🏠⚖️

After a grandfather's death, his property in Chennai was divided among his heirs by a compromise decree in 1994, and the father got a share of 1,811 sq. ft. In 1997 the father sold it. His minor son, born in January 1994, filed a suit through his mother claiming a half share, saying he had a right in that property by birth.

The Supreme Court disagreed. Under Section 8 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, the property passes to the Class I heirs, and a son's son is not a Class I heir of his grandfather. The father took his share in his own individual capacity, not as joint family property for his son. Once the property was partitioned, each heir's share became that heir's exclusive property, which he was free to sell. Section 6 did not help, because the son was not born when the grandfather died. The Court followed its earlier rulings in Chander Sen (1986) and Yudhishter (1987) and dismissed the appeal.

Keep in mind: this does not mean grandsons can never have rights in family property. The answer depends on how the property came to the father. Before buying, selling or claiming family property, have the title documents checked properly.

Case: J. Rajesh (Minor) v. M. Kumaran (Dead) through LRs, Civil Appeal No. 7020 of 2012, 2026 INSC 1208
Bench: Justices Sunita Agarwal and Augustine George Masih, Supreme Court, 6 Nov 2026
